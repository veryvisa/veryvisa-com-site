Low-income years are the most valuable

Many people stop working in their early sixties and have several low-income years before OAS starts and RRIF withdrawals become mandatory. Taking more from the RRSP in those years, at lower tax rates, can hold down income when withdrawals are later required and reduce the chance of OAS recovery.
Consider this together with other benefits. People with very low income may receive the Guaranteed Income Supplement (GIS), which shrinks as income rises, so RRSP withdrawals reduce it too. For whom drawing on the RRSP first is better depends on the whole family's income over the next few years.

Look first: List both spouses' expected income for the next ten or more years.

Easy to miss: Taking more from an RRSP can also reduce income-tested supplements.

Next step: Before filing each year, adjust again to that year's income.

Takeaway: The withdrawal order decides a lifetime's tax, not just this year's.

A couple's income can be evened out

Report part of a pension on the spouse's return

  1. Eligible pension income
  2. Joint election
  3. Both spouses' tax brackets
  4. Whether the recovery tax shrinks

Look first: Confirm which income counts as eligible pension income.

Easy to miss: CPP is not part of this split; sharing it needs a separate application.

Next step: The share can be chosen again each year; it need not be fixed.

Takeaway: Split well, and both spouses' tax and the recovery tax may fall together.

Common situations

One spouse has an employer pension and the other has very little income. Moving part of the eligible pension income to the other puts both in lower brackets, and the higher earner's OAS recovery may also shrink.
You retired early with a large RRSP. Taking somewhat more each year in the low-income years before OAS starts means smaller required withdrawals after seventy-one.
You need a large sum for a car or renovation. Using the TFSA first does not raise that year's income; one large RRIF withdrawal may raise both that year's tax and OAS recovery.
